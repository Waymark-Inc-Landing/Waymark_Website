How to rebuild trust in a marriage

Six honest steps, taken in order

Waymark Inc. · November 27, 2026

“Bear with one another and forgive one another… as the Lord has forgiven you.”

Colossians 3:13

When trust breaks in a marriage, both people usually want the same thing: for it to stop hurting. One wants to be believed again. The other wants to feel safe again. Neither happens because of a single conversation.

Trust is rebuilt the way it was built the first time, by what a person does again and again. Here is the order we have seen work.

Six steps

Tell the whole truth, once. Trust cannot grow on a partial story. Each new detail that surfaces later starts the clock over.

End what broke it. Whatever it was, a relationship, a habit, a hidden account, it has to stop completely and visibly.

Take responsibility without “but.” An apology that explains why the other person shares the blame is not yet an apology.

Offer openness before it is asked for. The one who broke trust volunteers where they are, who they are with and what they are spending. This is temporary, and it is what makes belief possible again.

Give the hurt time. The spouse who was hurt will ask the same questions more than once. Patient answers are part of the repair.

Keep small promises. Being home when you said you would be matters more than a grand gesture. Trust returns through ordinary days.

Forgiveness and trust are not the same thing

Forgiveness is a decision one person makes before God, and it can be given right away. Trust is a response to evidence, and it takes time. A spouse can truly forgive and still need months before they feel safe. That is not a failure of faith.

When to ask for help

Most couples cannot do this alone, because every conversation about it turns into the same fight. A third person keeps the conversation honest and moving. Dr. Jim Talley spent more than fifty years walking couples through this, and his work is the ground we stand on.

If there is violence or you are afraid, safety comes before any of these steps. In the United States, call the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233.
